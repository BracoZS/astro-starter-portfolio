I really love dogs. I think that they're pretty amazing greatures. I would say that my other (better) half is a dog person, but he only really likes some dog. He always remarks, however, on the fact that I generally adopt a position of thinking most dogs are great until proven otherwise.

If you’ve ever had the pleasure of training a dog - not just teaching it to walk on a lead and not shit on the floor - you’ll know it’s both deeply rewarding but also endlessly frustrating, because while we like to anthropomorphise our four legged friends, dogs motivations are different to ours and so their behaviour can sometimes be a little unpredicatble. Sure, they want food and they’d prefer not to be punished (fair), but beyond that, their priorities and motiviations are simultaneously obvious and equally unknowable, and so their behaviours and responses to what we think are perfectly logical cues, can be unexpected.

Consider a few examples. My dog often wants to sniff a fence post because another dog pissed on it, despite the fact that I'm offering him delicious treats. He'd rather eat his own spew than go hungry. For reasons known only to his kiwi-fruit-sized brain, he lies down every time he sees another working breed. Is it excitement? Instinct? A personal quirk? No idea. This unpredictability of dogs is likely a result of fundamentally different drivers to what we as civilised [citation needed] humans want.

Training a dog is as much about shaping desirable behaviour as it is about discouraging the unwanted. But there's a real twist; sometimes what we think is discouragement is actually encouragement in disguise and vice-versa. Recall your dog while off lead and he may not come back immediately...because something more interesting is happening. You could punish him for the delay, but that only teaches him that coming back is a terrible idea. Reward him when he eventually wanders over, though, and congratulations: you’ve just incentivised procrastination. He gets to muck around and earn a treat. Double win!

So you discover that you have to shape behaviour properly. Reward immediate recall. Reward immediate attention. Be consistent. Be clear. And above all, understand what the dog actually wants. You can’t assume he understands your intentions any more than you can fully understand his. When you're not getting the outcome that you want, take a breath and think about what things you might be unintentionally incentivising that are more appealing than what you're actually trying to achieve.

Ultimately, people aren’t all that different - they're often hard at work, quietly optimising for completely different outcomes to you. This doesn’t meanyou're opposed to eachother; it just means that misaligned incentives inevitably create friction. In any organisation, the ideal state is one where this type of misalignment is recognised early and addressed through deliberate cross-levelling and planning. The more common reality though is that you end up with the corporate equivalent of a dog barking at the nighbours doorbell: some confusion, not a lot of activity, very little understanding, and no real progress.

The fact remains that life simply isn't neat enough or agile enough to enact organisational change every time there is a tension in incentives. Instead, individuals become the meat in the sandwich, having to mediate between different priorities, find workable solutions, and all the while, reporting up their respective chains of command about how they're progressing toward their directed outcomes.

This is where I get to the point. Treat your coworkers like a dog. That is to say, if you're not getting the outcomes from coworkers that you want, if may be helpful to examine the way that you interact with them, with a view to understanding what they are actually optimising for. It is likely that there is a way to achieve a mutually satisfactory outcome (perhaps not always a mutually ideal or optimal one) by taking the time to understand what it is that your coworkers are actually seeking to achieve as their primary purpose. 

What part of they organisational goal are they directly contributing to? Are you asking them to de-prioritise their main effort to support yours? (Are you asking them to stop sniffing the pee, to come and cross the road safely?) If so, perhaps you need to revisit how you demonstrate that what you are asking of them is actually more important or valuable to them.

With a more satisfactory understanding of what others are trying to achieve, you can direct your efforts to your own behaviours and making sure that what you are doing is not inadvertently dis-incentivising collaboration. In other words, give people what they want (whether they can articulate it or not) to maximise the chances of compliance.

Now, what I'm proposing is not some form of workplace coercion or manipulation, though it would be easy to see it that way. Rather it is the true spirit of collaboration. Actually taking the time to understand other people's motivations and drivers in the workplace in order to shape both your and their behaviour to a more optimal outcome. A challenge, but one which, when viewed through a lens of understanding and humility ultimately leads to more productive outcomes.

The challenge is in the understanding, the asking, and in having the empathy to realise that there are multiple perspectives at play. In a corporate environment that doesn't prioritise or meaningfully empower teams to collaborate, there is a big leap of faith involved. Attitudes and culture change very slowly, and the road seldom travelled can be lonely and frustrating. 

On the odd occasion that it is effective and leads to superior outcomes, it's absolutely worth it.

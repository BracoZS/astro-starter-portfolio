I suspect that you, dear reader, are one of very few people to read this. And I’m absolutely okay with that. Given the amount that I write every day at work (mostly in the form of perfunctory emails), it’s a very odd sensation to write something that seems to be trying to make a point but with no audience in mind. Who am I convincing of anything? I suspect, precisely no one. Writing my thoughts on this slightly narcissistic website is far less about me telling other people things, and far more about critical thinking, practicing the skill of writing, and just working through ideas in a way that makes them tangible.

Something I’ve noticed about thinking, talking and writing, is that I find it far easier to work through problems in a way that is robust and defensible when I’m forced to put them down on paper or into a more concrete form. 

In terms of robustness of thought, there is a very clear order (least to most) for me which runs mental, verbal, written. I find it very easy to be convinced of my ideas at first blush when formulating them in my head. Often, however, it is not until I verbalise my thoughts to another person that I realise that they are only half formed and need more substance, refinement or rework. At the speed of thought, it is all too easy to rush over assumptions, rationalisations, and conclusions, or miss important links between ideas and arguments. Speaking them aloud forces you to consolidate what may be purely theoretical or conceptual into language. The act of formalising in words make the ideas somehow more concrete. For better or worse, it also reveals many of the flaws in the argument.

It is still fairly easy, though, to rush an argument together when verbalising it and miss an assumption, tenuous logical link or fallacy, and never return to it. While expressing ideas in words does indeed force you to pin them down, it is only fleetingly so. The moment those words are gone, there is no guarantee you can return to them in the way they were. Memory is such a fickle thing. Bamboozle, bluster or otherwise rush a discussion and the errors are missed and fall into an impenetrable black hole of assumptions, never to return and be revisited (until it’s too late). In writing though, logic is set forth with nowhere to hide. Becasue of this, eleagance, simplicity, clarity and precision in language come into their own. It may be fine to ramble at length verbally to convince someone of an argument, but to do so effectively in writing is something else entirely. The written form is comparatively colder and less unforgiving and it demands structure, rigour and clarity that can be forgiven in speech. It requires a writer to be convincing, thorough and compelling – characteristics that appear to be less and less common as time passes.

I’ve realised recently that this type of compelling and convincing writing is not a luxury, it is a real an essential skill for anyone with a desire to make an impact in the world. While writing remains an essential function in many workplaces, weaving a narrative with finesse in order to make a compelling point is an entirely different skill to sending efficient and effective transactional emails. While I can’t argue against the utility and necessity of the every day mundane emails that keep business running – they are sadly a necessary part of professional life – the omnipresence of this type of writing (sadly) embeds that style of writing as a professional default. 

So, while being able to transact is necessary, treating serious discussions that require depth of thought and nuance as transactional exchanges is clearly inappropriate. Correspondingly, to not act with intellectual rigour, purely because every day business demands brevity and succinctness is inexcusable. The true art, therefore, is to exercise rigour while being communicative, expressive, compelling, convincing and succinct. This is a challenge that demands not just intuition, but practice and deliberate effort.

This brings me back to the start. Why would I write something, knowing that almost no one will read it (in fact, I rather hope that few will). Consider it practice for the sake of practice, or practice for the sake of self improvement. Much like going to a gym, or joining a sporting club with no intention of competing seriously, or taking a skiing lesson just so you can get better at skiing, practicing the art of writing is to seek self improvement for the sake of self improvement. 

So, fictional reader, consider this an effort to practice a skill that is underappreciated and undervalued. One never wants to be forced to learn a skill at the point they most need to use it. Rather, you learn the skill in advance, knowing it will be useful at some indeterminate time in the future. I write to become a better writer, and not necessarily to make a point. At least,  not to make a point now, but to ensure that when I need to make a point, I am not left wanting.
